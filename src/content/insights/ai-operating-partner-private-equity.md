An AI operating partner is the person a fund holds accountable for turning AI from a paragraph in the investment memo into money in the portfolio's P&L. That covers the whole hold: a view before the deal signs, a plan per company after it closes, working systems in the middle, and evidence a buyer will accept at exit. If the person in the seat can't point to something running in production, they aren't doing this job.

The title is spreading faster than the definition. [Korn Ferry](https://www.kornferry.com/institute/the-ai-operating-partner-the-latest-pe-portfolio-value-creation-role) notes that large PE firms have created AI operating partner roles, filled either by full-time employees or by part-time advisers. In practice the same title covers a former CIO running an IT forum, a data scientist building deal-team dashboards and a consultant with a good slide library. Useful people, doing a different job. This piece sets out what that job contains, how it differs from its neighbours, which structure suits which fund, and how to judge it in year one.

## Why funds are creating the role now

GP expectations and portfolio reality have drifted apart. The [Bain & Company and StepStone Group 2026 Private Equity GP Outlook](https://www.bain.com/insights/private-equitys-reality-check-gp-outlook-2026/) found GPs report their highest returns from generative AI in their own deal sourcing and due diligence. Inside portfolio companies, the benefits skew to cost savings, and nearly 40% of GPs don't expect material financial impact from AI in their portfolio companies in 2026.

So the fund's own team uses AI well. The companies it owns, mostly, don't yet.

[FTI Consulting's 2026 Private Equity AI Radar](https://www.fticonsulting.com/insights/reports/2026-private-equity-ai-radar) puts a number on the gap. Only 36% of portfolio companies deploy AI in production with real operational impact, and just 7% of respondents describe AI as fully integrated across the portfolio. The top constraint to scaling, named by 35%, is talent.

That last figure is why the role exists. Funds have plenty of ideas and portfolio companies have plenty of licences. What's scarce is people who can walk into a £60m distribution business, find the three workflows worth automating, get them built and keep them running. Most portfolio companies can't hire that person and wouldn't keep them busy if they did. A fund can hold one across the portfolio.

## What an AI operating partner actually does, from diligence to exit

The job has seven parts. Funds that write a role description covering only two or three of them tend to get someone who produces plans and not much else.

Before a deal signs, the operating partner gives the deal team a view on two questions. How exposed is this business to AI, and what could AI realistically add to the plan? That means reading the product and the data, testing any AI claims in the information memorandum, and sizing a handful of cost and revenue opportunities firmly enough to go into the model. It's lighter than full [AI due diligence](/ai-due-diligence), and a good operating partner knows when to call for the heavier version.

After close, the first job is a portfolio-wide AI baseline. For each company: what's in use, what's actually in production, what data is accessible, who owns technology, and how many hours a month go into the repetitive work AI is now good at. Without a baseline, nothing measured later means anything.

From the baseline comes a plan per company. Three to five named workflows, each with an owner, a cost, a timeline and a number in hours or pounds. I've set out how to build one in [the AI value-creation plan](/insights/ai-value-creation-plan). Tie it to the 100-day plan and the budget, or it drifts.

Then the operating partner becomes the counterpart to each CEO. A monthly session, same agenda every time: what shipped, what's stuck, what's next, what help is needed. The CEO needs someone who has sat in the chair and will say plainly that the pilot the head of operations loves isn't worth finishing.

The fifth part is the one most role descriptions leave out. Delivery capacity. A company with no engineering team, or one fully booked on the core product, will not ship an AI workflow because a plan says it should. The operating partner either builds it alongside the team or brings in people who can, on fixed scopes, and makes sure it's handed over properly. Plans without hands are how funds end up with a folder of roadmaps.

Every quarter, the operating partner reports to the investment committee. Across the portfolio: what's live, what it's returning, what's been stopped and why, and what next quarter commits to. One page per company, same format each time. [Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/field-notes-from-generative-ai-insurgency-global-private-equity-report-2025/) describes Vista Equity Partners asking portfolio companies to submit goals and quantified benefits for GenAI initiatives. That's the discipline worth copying: quantified commitments, reviewed on a rhythm.

Finally, exit evidence. A buyer's diligence team will discount anything described as "AI-enabled" that can't be shown working. The operating partner builds the evidence pack through the hold: systems in production, usage logs, before and after measures, the cost to run them, and who maintains them. Evidenced, it goes into the equity story. Asserted, it gets marked down.

## How it differs from the roles it gets confused with

Four alternatives come up in almost every conversation I have with funds. Each does something real. None covers the whole job.

| Alternative | What it does well | What it usually doesn't do |
|---|---|---|
| Technology operating partner | Infrastructure, ERP, cyber, IT cost, carve-outs | Redesign workflows around AI or build AI systems in production |
| CTO or CIO network | Peer learning, shared vendor terms, benchmarking | Own outcomes or deliver anything inside a specific company |
| Big Four AI strategy engagement | Structured assessment, board-ready materials, breadth | Stay for the hold, build, or answer for results a year later |
| Vendor partnerships | Discounted tools, implementation help for their own product | Give neutral advice on whether you need the product at all |

The technology operating partner is the closest cousin and the most common source of confusion. A good one keeps systems secure, integrated and cheap to run. The AI job sits closer to operations than to IT. It starts with how work gets done and which hours can be returned, and the systems follow. Some people do both well. Most are much better at one, and funds should hire for the one they need.

Vendor partnerships deserve one more line. They're fine as a procurement tool and poor as a source of judgement, because the vendor's answer to every problem is its product.

## Full-time or fractional

Korn Ferry's split between full-time employee and part-time adviser is the right first decision. It turns on portfolio count, how much of the portfolio is software, and whether the fund wants to build an internal team or retain a capability. My working view:

- More than about 20 active portfolio companies, a heavy software weighting, or a thesis that leans on AI in every deal: a full-time AI operating partner, who will soon want a small team underneath.
- 6 to 15 companies and a small operating bench: a fractional AI operating partner at 2 to 4 days a month across the portfolio, with delivery bought on fixed scopes when a company needs it.
- Fewer than about 5 companies, or a single large asset: put the capability inside the company, through an interim leader or an embedded delivery team.

I've covered the economics of the full-time hire at mid-market funds [in a separate piece](/insights/why-mid-market-funds-cant-hire-an-ai-operating-partner), so I won't repeat them here. The more useful question is what the fractional version must still contain. Days per month is the easy part to agree. The real test is whether the arrangement still covers all seven parts above, particularly delivery capacity and quarterly IC reporting. A part-time adviser who attends a few board meetings is a different, cheaper and much less useful thing.

## What a good one has done before

Three things, and I'd want evidence for each.

They have built and shipped AI systems that run in production in real businesses, and can show you what's still running six months on. Prototypes and hackathon demos don't count. Making a workflow survive messy data, a sceptical finance team and UK GDPR takes different skills from making one look good in a meeting.

They have sat in an operator's chair. Run a P&L, managed a team, had to choose between the AI project and hitting the quarter. That's what earns a CEO's trust, and it's what stops them recommending things a management team can't absorb.

And they can talk to a CFO about EBITDA. Every recommendation should arrive with the arithmetic: hours, loaded cost, run cost, payback. Someone who talks about "productivity" without a number will struggle at IC.

The interview questions I'd ask a candidate:

1. Show me a system you built that is still running unattended. What does it do, what does it cost to run, and who maintains it?
2. Tell me about an AI project you stopped. How did you decide, and how long did it take?
3. How would you baseline one of our portfolio companies in the first month? What data would you ask for?
4. Size the EBITDA effect of automating invoice matching at a £40m business. Do the arithmetic out loud.
5. What would you do in a company with no engineering team and a sceptical CEO?
6. Do you receive commission, referral fees or equity from any software vendor? Which ones?
7. What would you put in front of our IC at the end of your first quarter?

Question 4 usually tells you most of what you need to know.

## How to measure an AI operating partner in year one

Measure outcomes, not activity. Workshops held and licences rolled out are inputs. The year one scorecard I'd set:

- A baseline for every portfolio company within 90 days, signed off by each CEO.
- A costed plan agreed with each management team by month six, with named owners.
- Workflows live in production, and how many are still running unattended six months after launch.
- Hours returned to the business per month, by company, measured against the baseline.
- Annualised EBITDA effect net of run costs, agreed with each CFO rather than claimed by the operating partner.
- Projects stopped, and how quickly. A year with no kills means nobody was looking.

Here is what one line of that scorecard looks like, as an illustration. Take a £40m services business with 120 staff. The baseline finds around 1,500 hours a month going into three workflows: proposal drafting, invoice matching and first-line customer queries. By month nine, systems in production are returning 600 of those hours. At a loaded cost of £35 an hour, that's £21,000 a month, or £252,000 a year. Take off £30,000 a year to run and maintain the systems and you have £222,000.

That becomes EBITDA only if the business does something with the hours: slower hiring, absorbed growth, roles not back-filled. Hours returned and money saved are different numbers, and a good operating partner reports both. If the £222,000 is real, at an illustrative 9x exit multiple it's worth roughly £2m of enterprise value from one company. Repeat that across a portfolio and the role pays for itself several times over.

## A sample first 90 days

The first month is listening and counting. Meet every CEO and CFO. Read the diligence reports and 100-day plans. Run the baseline in each company. Agree the IC reporting template with the fund, so the first report isn't a surprise to anyone. Pick one small, visible win per company that could be live within six weeks.

The second month is choosing. Draft each company's plan with the arithmetic attached. Stop or pause pilots that won't pay back. Check the data protection position, including DPIAs under UK GDPR, and EU AI Act exposure for any company selling into the EU. Start delivery on the two or three highest-value workflows in companies that can't build them alone.

The third month is proof. The first small wins are live and measured against the baseline. Each management team has signed off its plan. The first IC report goes in: baseline, plans, what's live, what's stopped, and what next quarter commits to. By day 90 the fund should have seen a few things actually working.

## Red flags when hiring or retaining one

Tool evangelism is the most common. Every problem, in every company, ends with the same product. Sometimes the right answer is a spreadsheet and a better process.

Vendor commissions are the most corrosive. Referral fees and reseller margins are legal and common, and they bend advice. Ask directly, get the answer in writing, and make disclosure a term of the engagement.

Decks without deliveries are the easiest to spot and the easiest to excuse. After two quarters, count what's live. If the answer is mostly strategy documents and "phase two", the role has become the Big Four engagement it was meant to replace.

A few quieter ones. Reports that measure activity rather than outcomes. A reluctance to stop anything. No baseline, so every number is a claim. Plenty of time with the CEO and none with the finance team. Review the role at six and twelve months against the scorecard above, and be willing to change the person or the structure.

The simplest test I know is the house one: what is still running, unattended, six months later? A good AI operating partner will hand you a list. Everyone else will hand you a deck.
