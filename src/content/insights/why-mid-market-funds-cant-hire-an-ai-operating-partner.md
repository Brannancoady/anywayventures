Large-cap funds are building AI operating teams. Lower-mid-market funds — six to fifteen portfolio companies, small operating bench — mostly aren't, not because they don't see the need but because the economics don't work. A credible AI operating partner costs what a partner costs, and the portfolio isn't large enough to amortise that.

The workaround most funds have landed on is a workshop: bring in a consultancy, run an AI strategy day with each management team, produce a roadmap. Six months later very little is in production, because a roadmap is not a capability and management teams were already busy.

The structure that does work is fractional and continuous: a retained operating partner two to four days a month across the portfolio, who writes each company's AI value-creation plan, meets each CEO monthly, reports to the IC quarterly — and, crucially, can bring hands when a portco can't deliver alone. Plans without delivery capacity are how the workshop failure repeats.

The fund gets a single owner for the AI thesis across the hold, at a fraction of a full-time cost, with patterns that transfer from one portco to the next. The portfolio companies get a counterpart who has sat in their chair. And the exit story gets evidenced rather than asserted.
