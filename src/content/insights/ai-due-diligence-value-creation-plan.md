Technical diligence has a settled shape: architecture, code quality, security, team, a red/amber/green summary. It is useful and it is almost entirely defensive — a list of things that could go wrong. AI has been bolted onto that list as another risk category: is the target's AI real, is the data clean, is there IP exposure.

That misses the point of the moment. For most mid-market targets the AI question is not 'what could go wrong' but 'how much cost comes out of this operation, how much faster does it grow, and how quickly can a new owner make that happen'. That is a value-creation question, and it belongs in the investment case, not the risk appendix.

Done properly it looks like a function-by-function walk of the business — finance, operations, sales, service, product — ranked by hours and margin at stake, tested against what the target's data and systems can actually support today, and sequenced into a first-hundred-days plan with a conservative, base and stretch case the deal team can drop into the model.

The uncomfortable part is that it can only be written by someone who has recently built these systems in production. Vendor decks and analyst frameworks will tell you what is theoretically possible. The plan you can underwrite is the one that knows what broke last month.
